How to Plan a Budget Hot Springs Trip Without the Stress

A hot springs weekend does not need a resort price tag. Free wild pools, ten-dollar town baths, and a little planning can buy you the same steam for a fraction of the cost.

By Clara Bennett · November 9, 2026 · 11 min read

The first time I priced a hot springs weekend, I nearly talked myself out of it. The resort websites showed infinity pools, robe service, and day passes pricier than my grocery bill. Then a friend sent a photo from a free pool in Oregon: a ring of rocks, steam curling off the water, nobody else for a mile. The soak in that photo cost nothing, and the lesson shapes every trip since. The water is the same temperature whether you pay nothing for it or seventy dollars. What changes is the scenery, the crowd, and the plumbing. Choose those carefully, and a hot springs weekend can cost less than a nice dinner out.

This guide covers the whole plan: picking the right kind of spring for your budget, timing your visit, packing right, behaving well enough to be welcomed back, and a checklist that keeps the trip from unraveling. Prices shift by season, so treat the numbers here as a map, not a menu, and check the current rate before you drive.

Pick your kind of spring: wild, municipal, or resort

Hot springs come in three rough tiers, and the price gap between them is enormous. Wild springs sit on public land, usually Forest Service or Bureau of Land Management ground, with no facilities beyond a trail and a rock-ringed pool. Many are free. Umpqua in Oregon, Fifth Water in Utah, and Deep Creek in California ask nothing at the water, and Travertine Hot Springs in California runs free BLM pools at 100 to 106 degrees minutes off the highway. The trade is effort and uncertainty: you may hike in, the temperature varies with the weather, and there is no one to ask for help.

Municipal and state pools are the sweet spot for most first trips: town or state-park facilities with changing rooms and posted temperatures, at the lowest entry fees of any developed site. Grover Hot Springs, a California state park pool held at 102 to 104 degrees, charges ten dollars for an adult and five for a child, plus eight dollars per vehicle. Keough Hot Springs near Bishop runs a big pool at 88 to 90 degrees with a 104-degree hot pool alongside, at twelve dollars for adults and seven for children. Ouray Hot Springs Pool in Colorado, Lava Hot Springs in Idaho, and Hot Springs State Park in Thermopolis, Wyoming, sit in the same affordable band. Hot Springs National Park in Arkansas charges no entrance fee at all, though the bathhouses inside do.

Resorts are the top tier, and the prices show it. Mount Princeton in Colorado runs about forty dollars for a day in the historic bathhouse, with a twenty-dollar evening entry Monday through Wednesday. The Springs Resort in Pagosa Springs charges around seventy-four dollars per adult, and Iron Mountain Hot Springs lists family admission from forty-three to seventy-five dollars. Valley View Hot Springs in Colorado sits in the middle at roughly twenty dollars for a day pass, with overnight stays around forty dollars per person. Resorts buy you certainty: posted temperatures, clean facilities, food on site, and a bed nearby. If the trip is a special occasion, that certainty is worth paying for. If it is a Tuesday, it is not.

When to go: seasons, weekdays, and the crowd clock

Timing is the cheapest discount in hot springs travel. Summer weekends are the most crowded and the most expensive at resorts, while weekday visits in the shoulder seasons, late spring and early fall, bring lower prices, easier parking, and quieter pools. Winter is the secret season: soaking in 104-degree water while snow falls is the best argument for going in January, and many resorts drop their rates once the holiday crowds leave. Wild springs are best in cool weather too, since summer heat makes a hot pool feel like work.

Book ahead for anything popular. Mount Princeton and similar resorts sell timed entries that fill up, and a sold-out Saturday is a wasted drive. For wild springs, check the land agency's page before you go: closures happen for fire, flooding, and trail work, and one popular California spring is closed under a Forest Service order until June 2027. A five-minute check beats a four-hour drive to a locked gate. If you are flexible, arrive on a weekday and soak in the late afternoon when day visitors leave.

The money plan: what a weekend actually costs

A budget hot springs weekend has four line items: getting there, getting in, sleeping, and eating. Gas is usually the biggest, so pick a spring within a half day's drive of home. Entry is the easiest to control: a wild spring costs nothing, a town pool ten to twenty dollars, a resort forty to seventy-five. Sleeping is where budgets die, so skip the resort room for Forest Service campgrounds near the springs, about ten to nineteen dollars a night, or a simple motel in the nearest town. Food is the quiet leak: pack breakfast and lunch, bring a cooler, and save the restaurant money for one good dinner.

Here is a realistic shape for two people, one night, at a municipal pool: gas and parking, forty dollars; pool entry, twenty; a campsite, fifteen; groceries and one dinner out, sixty. That is roughly one hundred and thirty-five dollars for a full weekend of soaking, and the wild-spring version drops to under a hundred. The resort version of the same weekend can pass five hundred before you have eaten. None of these numbers is a promise, since prices move, but the ratio holds: the water is the cheap part, and everything around it is the choice.

What to pack, and what to leave behind

Packing for a hot springs trip is a short list, because the nearest store may be an hour away. Bring two towels, one for the pool and one dry one for the car: a wet towel in a cold car is a miserable end to a good soak. Bring a water bottle and keep it at the pool edge: hot water pulls fluid out through sweat you never feel, and dehydration is the most common way a soak goes wrong. Flip-flops matter more than you think, because pool decks and rocky trails are slippery and cold.

A pocket thermometer lets you check a wild pool before you trust it with your skin, which matters when source water can run far hotter than the pool. A dry bag keeps your phone and keys safe from steam and splashes, and layers for after the soak are essential: your body cools fast once you leave the water, and a warm hat and a fleece turn the walk back to camp into a pleasure. Leave behind the glass bottles, the Bluetooth speaker, and the scented lotions. Glass breaks near pools, music ruins the quiet that other soakers came for, and lotions wash off into water that other people are sitting in.

Etiquette: the rules that keep you welcome

Hot springs run on manners, and the manners change with the place, so the first rule is to read the signs and follow them. At developed pools, shower before you get in. In Iceland, that shower is thorough, with soap and without your swimsuit, and attendants do check. In Japan, the onsen ritual is stricter still: wash sitting at the low showers, rinse until no soap remains, and never bring soap into the bath, since the bath is for soaking only. In the United States, the rule is simpler: shower first, rinse off sunscreen and lotion, and never use soap in a natural pool, since the water drains straight into the surrounding ecosystem.

Swimwear rules vary more than visitors expect. Most American pools require swimsuits, and many wild springs do too, while some remote pools are clothing optional by local custom, and a few resorts set different rules after dark. Check the posted rule for the specific spring. Keep your voice low, because sound carries across still water and the person in the next pool came for quiet. Leave no trace: pack out everything you brought, including food scraps and hair ties, and take a bag for litter someone else left. No cameras at clothing-optional springs, ever, and ask before photographing anyone near a pool.

The budget hot springs checklist

  • Choose: wild (free), municipal (ten to twenty dollars), or resort (forty to seventy-five). Match the tier to the trip, not the fantasy.
  • Check: current prices, hours, and closures on the operator or land agency page before you drive.
  • Book: timed entries and campsites ahead for popular spots; weekday visits are cheaper and quieter.
  • Pack: two towels, water bottle, flip-flops, dry bag, pocket thermometer, warm layers, snacks.
  • Budget: gas, entry, sleep, and food, with one restaurant meal as the treat, not the plan.
  • Behave: shower first, no soap in natural pools, follow the swimwear rule, keep it quiet, pack out all trash.
  • Soak smart: short rounds, cool-down breaks, head above water, no alcohol before or during.

A sample weekend, start to finish

Here is the shape of a trip that works, built around a municipal pool a few hours from home. Friday evening, drive out after work, set up camp before dark, or check into the cheap motel in town. Saturday morning, sleep in, eat breakfast at camp, and wander until mid-afternoon, when the day visitors start leaving. Soak from late afternoon into the evening, in short rounds with cool-down breaks, then eat the one good dinner you budgeted for. Sunday, take a slow morning soak if the pool opens early, pack up without rushing, and drive home in daylight.

That schedule is deliberately light: the point of a hot springs weekend is not to fill it. The water does the work, and the rest of the trip exists to get you there and home again without stress. If the weather turns or the pool is crowded, the plan bends: a second town pool, a longer hike, an earlier dinner. A budget trip survives surprises because there is not much money at stake, and that is the real luxury of going cheap.

Before your first soak, it is worth knowing what the water does to your body and where the real risks sit, so read up on the health benefits and risks of soaking first. Then pick a spring within driving distance, pack the short list, and go. The steam, the quiet, and the loose-limbed drive home are waiting, and they cost far less than the brochures suggest.
