Hot Springs and Your Health: The Real Benefits (and the Risks) of Soaking

Warm mineral water feels like medicine, and sometimes it is. Here is what the evidence actually supports, who should stay out of the pool, and how to soak without harming yourself.

By Clara Bennett · November 9, 2026 · 11 min read

My first real hot spring was a shallow pool in the woods, steam rising in the cold morning air, and I stayed in far longer than I should have. When I finally climbed out, my legs felt loose in a way a hot shower had never managed, and I slept like a stone. I came home convinced the water had healed something. A few years and several soaks later, my view is more careful: hot springs can help, in specific ways, for specific people, and they can also hurt you if you treat them like a bathtub. This is the guide I wish I had read before that first long soak.

Start with the honest summary. Warm water immersion does real things to a human body: it relaxes muscles, eases stiff joints, lowers the load on sore knees and hips, and calms the nervous system. Some of that is backed by decent trials, especially for osteoarthritis and chronic back pain. Some of it is softer evidence, and some of what resorts claim about minerals is mostly marketing. The risks are real too, from overheating to scalding water and a rare brain infection. None of this means you should stay away. It means you should soak like a grown-up: informed, hydrated, and out before you feel dizzy.

What warm water actually does to your body

Three forces do the work in a hot spring, and none of them is magic. Heat dilates blood vessels, which increases circulation to muscles and skin and helps tight tissue let go. Buoyancy unloads your joints: in a 2017 study using instrumented hip and knee implants, researchers measured joint contact forces 36 to 55 percent lower in water than on land for the same movements. Stillness matters too. Sitting chest deep in warm water nudges the parasympathetic nervous system, the rest-and-digest branch, which is why an hour in a pool can feel like a nap you took while awake.

The minerals get most of the credit in brochures, and they deserve the least. Hot spring water carries sulfur, calcium, magnesium, and silica, and sulfur is the one you will smell first. There is little evidence that these minerals pass through skin in amounts that change your health. What the heat, the buoyancy, and the quiet do is measurable. What the minerals do is mostly the smell, the soft feel of the water, and a story that sells rooms. But if a spring claims its water cures a named disease, treat the claim like any advertisement: politely, and at arm's length.

What the research supports, and where it stops

Joint pain has the cleanest evidence: a randomized trial of 73 women over 65 with knee osteoarthritis found heated-pool hydrotherapy twice a week for six weeks beat education alone on pain, function, and measured strength. A 2026 study in Frontiers in Medicine followed 66 patients with mild to moderate knee osteoarthritis through two weeks of balneotherapy; the group that added aquatic exercise saw pain drop 31 percent, against 13 percent for baths alone. In the Azores, a trial of 120 knee osteoarthritis patients found 45 of 60 bathers benefited, with improvements still measurable three months later.

Chronic low back pain has the deepest stack of trials: a 2015 review synthesized 33 randomized trials, and several found pain relief lasting up to six months after the course. Sleep is the most practical finding of all: a warm bath in the hour or two before bed helps people fall asleep faster, because the body cools down after leaving warm water, mimicking the natural drop in core temperature that triggers sleepiness. Soak in the evening, sleep better, wake up easier.

The honest limits matter as much as the wins. A Cochrane review of balneotherapy for rheumatoid arthritis rated the evidence very low quality and called it inconclusive, so nothing here speaks to that condition. Many balneotherapy trials are small, single-center, and unblinded, and sham controls using plain tap water are rare, leaving room for placebo effects. The trials support relief of pain and stiffness and support for mobility. They do not show that warm water treats or reverses osteoarthritis, replaces a knee replacement, or reduces the need for medication. Soaking is a useful tool, not a cure.

Heat, buoyancy, and stillness do the work. The minerals mostly do the smell.

Who should skip the soak, or ask a doctor first

Some bodies should not be in hot water, and the list is longer than resort websites admit. Pregnancy sits at the top. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises against hot tubs and saunas early in pregnancy, and the Consumer Product Safety Commission puts the pregnancy limit at 100 degrees Fahrenheit, below the 104 degree ceiling for everyone else. The reason is core temperature: a developing fetus is sensitive to heat, and a soak long enough to raise your temperature is a soak long enough to matter.

Heart disease is the second group to pause. Hot water dilates blood vessels and can swing blood pressure quickly, which is a poor combination with congestive heart failure or uncontrolled hypertension. People with low blood pressure face the same problem from the other direction: heat plus standing up out of the pool is a classic fainting setup. Diabetes deserves a conversation with a clinician too, because nerve damage can dull the sense of heat, so a diabetic soaker may not notice scalding water. Open wounds are a hard no in untreated natural water, since broken skin is an open door for bacteria. The CDC also advises keeping children under five out of hot tubs entirely, and each resort sets its own age rule, so check before you bring them.

The rare dangers are rare, and still worth respecting

Scalding is the most underestimated risk. Natural spring water emerges from the ground far hotter than any bath: the thermal water at Hot Springs National Park surfaces at an average of 143 degrees Fahrenheit, and the US Geological Survey notes that water above 150 degrees can cause third-degree burns in seconds. The Consumer Product Safety Commission says water at 140 degrees can burn skin in about six seconds, and more than 20 people have died from burns after entering Yellowstone's hot springs. Managed pools are kept between roughly 98 and 104 degrees, the CDC's maximum for hot tubs, but wild pools are not managed. Check the temperature with a thermometer, not your hand, and never enter water whose temperature you do not know. Before you drive out, a directory that lists each natural hot spring with verified temperatures and prices can save you a wasted trip and a dangerous guess.

The second danger is stranger and far rarer, but it shapes the one rule every soaker should memorize. Naegleria fowleri is an ameba that lives in warm fresh water, hot springs included. If water carrying it goes up the nose, it can cause a brain infection that is nearly always fatal: the CDC counts 167 US cases from 1962 to 2024, with four survivors. Its advice is blunt and applies to every hot spring, resort pool included: always keep your head above water. Do not dunk, do not dive, do not let children splash water into each other's faces. Poorly maintained springs can also harbor Legionella, the bacteria behind a severe respiratory illness, which is one more reason to favor springs with visible flow over stagnant, unloved pools.

How to soak safely, in practice

Safe soaking is mostly a matter of time, temperature, and honesty with yourself. Keep soaks to 15 or 20 minutes at a time, then get out, cool down, and drink water before going back in. Public spa rules in New Jersey and Illinois set a 15-minute limit at 100 to 104 degrees, and Washington DC says 10 to 15. No US health agency sets a formal time limit for natural springs, but the physics is the same: in a 1981 study, some women in 106-degree water reached a body temperature of 102 degrees, none in under ten minutes.

Hydrate before you get in, not after you feel thirsty, because hot water pulls fluid out through sweat you never notice. Bring a water bottle to the pool edge and use it between rounds. Never soak after drinking alcohol: the CPSC warns that the risk of drowning is significantly heightened when people drink before or during a hot soak, since alcohol and heat together drop blood pressure and dull judgment. Never soak alone in a very hot pool if you are prone to lightheadedness, and get out the moment you feel dizzy, nauseous, or strangely cold. Those are the exit signs.

The safe soak checklist

  • Temperature: 98 to 104 degrees Fahrenheit for soaking pools. Test with a thermometer, never your hand.
  • Time: 15 to 20 minutes per round, then cool down and hydrate before going back in.
  • Head: always above water, in every spring, resort pool included.
  • Water: drink before and between rounds. Keep a bottle at the pool edge.
  • Alcohol: none before or during a soak. It raises drowning risk sharply.
  • Skin: skip soaking with open wounds, rashes, or fresh tattoos.
  • Exit signs: dizziness, nausea, or feeling cold mean get out now, not in five minutes.

After the soak: cool down slowly, then rest

The hour after a soak deserves as much care as the soak itself. Rinse off in a shower if one is available, especially after sulfur-rich water, which can dry skin and stain light swimwear. Drink water again, eat something with salt in it, and give your body time to cool before driving a long distance. An evening soak pairs beautifully with an early night, and the deep sleep that follows is the closest thing to a proven benefit hot springs offer. If you feel wiped out the next day, you probably soaked too long or too hot, and the fix is shorter rounds next time, not avoiding the water altogether.

Hot springs are worth the trip, and the research gives you permission to enjoy them without pretending they are medicine. The heat loosens what is tight, the water carries what is heavy, and the quiet does the rest. Respect the temperature, respect the time, keep your head up, and the water will do what it has done for thousands of years: make an ordinary body feel briefly at ease. Once you know how to soak safely, the next question is where to go, and that is where planning a budget hot springs trip comes in, because the best soak in the world is the one you can actually afford to take.
